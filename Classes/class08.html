02 — The Journey

Evolution of Word Embeddings

Before diving into each model, it's crucial to understand the progression — each step solved a concrete limitation of the previous approach.

📖
PRE-2013
Bag of Words / TF-IDF
Count-based methods. Words are indices in a vocabulary. No semantic similarity — "cat" and "kitten" are orthogonal. High-dimensional sparse vectors. No notion of word order or context.
Sparse · No Semantics · No Order
⚡
2013–2017
Word2Vec / GloVe / FastText
Dense static embeddings. Capture semantic similarity (king − man + woman ≈ queen). But every word gets exactly ONE vector regardless of context. "Bank" → always the same 300-dim vector, whether it means a riverbank or a financial institution.
Dense · Semantic · Static (one vector per word)
🌊
2018 (Feb)
ELMo — Embeddings from Language Models
The first widely-adopted contextual embedding model. Uses deep bidirectional LSTMs — looks left AND right in a sentence. "Bank" now gets different vectors in different sentences. A major leap forward, but still LSTM-based (slow, sequential).
Contextual · BiLSTM · Character-level
🤗
2018 (Oct)
BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers
Replaced LSTMs with the Transformer encoder. Pre-trained on Masked Language Modeling + Next Sentence Prediction. Parallelisable, deeply bidirectional, and fine-tunable on any downstream task. Became the dominant NLP backbone for years and defined the modern era.
Contextual · Transformer · Bidirectional · Fine-tuneable
🔗
2019
Sentence-BERT (SBERT)
BERT is terrible at sentence similarity out-of-the-box (due to the [CLS] token pooling issue). Sentence-BERT fine-tunes BERT using a Siamese network with triplet loss to produce meaningful sentence-level embeddings that can be efficiently compared with cosine similarity.
Sentence-level · Siamese · Cosine-comparable
🚀
2020–Present
Modern SOTA Embeddings
E5, BGE, GTE, text-embedding-ada-002, Cohere Embed — massive embedding models trained on billions of pairs. Support multilingual, multi-task, long-context. But built on the same BERT/Transformer foundation introduced here.
Multilingual · Large-scale · Production-ready

The Core Problem with Static Embeddings

Static embeddings assign exactly one vector per word token, regardless of context. This breaks on polysemous words — words with multiple meanings:

🔴 Static Embedding Problem — same vector, different meanings
"I went to the bank to deposit money." → bank = [0.12, -0.45, 0.78 ...]
"We sat on the river bank at sunset." → bank = [0.12, -0.45, 0.78 ...]
"The bank approved our loan application." → bank = [0.12, -0.45, 0.78 ...]
🟢 Contextual Embedding Solution — different vectors, different meanings
"I went to the bank (financial)." → bank = [0.91, 0.12, -0.33 ...] ← finance context
"The river bank was muddy." → bank = [-0.44, 0.87, 0.21 ...] ← geography context
"The bank approved the loan." → bank = [0.76, 0.05, -0.61 ...] ← institution context

What Makes an Embedding "Contextual"?

A contextual embedding model reads the entire sentence before producing the vector for any given word. The embedding of each token is influenced by every other token in the sequence — so meaning is derived from context, not just the word itself.

🔍

Polysemy Handling

The same word gets different vectors in different contexts. Solves the bank/bank ambiguity problem completely.

📐

Syntax Awareness

"I ate the apple" vs "Apple released a new phone" — the word Apple gets completely different representations.

🔗

Long-Range Dependencies

Words far apart in a sentence can still influence each other's representations — impossible with static embeddings.

🎯

Transfer Learning

Pre-train once on huge corpus, then fine-tune on tiny labelled dataset for any downstream task. No task-specific architecture needed.

03 — First Contextual Model

ELMo — Embeddings from Language Models

🌊

ELMo

Peters et al. — Allen Institute for AI · February 2018

ELMo (Embeddings from Language Models) was the first breakthrough in contextual word representations. Rather than assigning a fixed vector per word, ELMo runs each word through a deep bidirectional LSTM and produces representations from ALL layers — weighted and summed according to the downstream task.

Architecture

ELMo uses a two-layer bidirectional LSTM (biLM) on top of a character-level CNN. The character CNN means ELMo can handle misspellings and out-of-vocabulary words that would defeat word-level tokenisation.

For each token, ELMo produces three representations: the character-CNN embedding (layer 0), and two biLSTM hidden states (layers 1 and 2). The final ELMo embedding is a task-specific weighted sum of all three — the weights are learned during fine-tuning.

⚠️

ELMo's key limitation: LSTMs are sequential — they process tokens one at a time. This makes ELMo slow to train and slow at inference compared to BERT's parallelisable Transformer architecture. Also, the bidirectionality is "shallow" — the forward and backward LSTMs are trained independently and only concatenated, not jointly attending to each other like BERT's self-attention does.

Strengths
  • First widely-used contextual embedding
  • Handles OOV words (character-level)
  • Layer-wise representations for tasks
  • Improved SOTA on 6 NLP benchmarks
Limitations
  • LSTM is sequential (slow)
  • Shallow bidirectionality
  • Largely superseded by BERT
  • Harder to fine-tune than BERT

Using ELMo in Practice

ELMo is available via AllenNLP and TensorFlow Hub. Today it's mostly used for comparison or legacy systems — new projects use BERT or SBERT instead.

python · ELMo 
# Option 1: AllenNLP (older but authoritative)
# !pip install allennlp
from allennlp.modules.elmo import Elmo, batch_to_ids

options_file = "https://allennlp.s3.amazonaws.com/models/elmo/2x4096_512_2048cnn_2xhighway/elmo_2x4096_512_2048cnn_2xhighway_options.json"
weight_file  = "https://allennlp.s3.amazonaws.com/models/elmo/2x4096_512_2048cnn_2xhighway/elmo_2x4096_512_2048cnn_2xhighway_weights.hdf5"

# num_output_representations = number of ELMo outputs to use
elmo = Elmo(options_file, weight_file, num_output_representations=2, dropout=0)

sentences = [["I", "went", "to", "the", "bank"],
             ["The", "river", "bank", "was", "muddy"]]

character_ids = batch_to_ids(sentences)
embeddings    = elmo(character_ids)

# embeddings['elmo_representations'][0] → layer 1 representation
# embeddings['elmo_representations'][1] → layer 2 representation
# Shape: (batch_size, seq_len, 1024)

layer1 = embeddings['elmo_representations'][0]
print(layer1.shape)  # torch.Size([2, 5, 1024])

# The embedding of "bank" in sentence 1 vs sentence 2:
bank_financial = layer1[0, 4, :]  # "bank" in "I went to the bank"
bank_river     = layer1[1, 2, :]  # "bank" in "The river bank"

04 — The Transformer Revolution

BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

🤗

BERT

Devlin, Chang, Lee, Toutanova — Google AI · October 2018

BERT replaced ELMo's LSTM backbone with the Transformer encoder, enabling true deep bidirectionality and parallelisable training. It was pre-trained on BookCorpus (800M words) and English Wikipedia (2.5B words) using two self-supervised objectives — and then fine-tuned on specific tasks, achieving new state-of-the-art on 11 NLP benchmarks at release.

BERT's Two Pre-Training Objectives

🎭

Masked Language Modeling (MLM)

15% of input tokens are randomly masked with [MASK]. The model must predict the original token from context. This forces truly bidirectional attention — unlike GPT which can only look left. Each token must attend to ALL other tokens to recover the masked one.

🔗

Next Sentence Prediction (NSP)

50% of the time, sentence B actually follows sentence A; 50% of the time it's a random sentence. The model must classify which case it is. Teaches the model to understand relationships between sentences — critical for tasks like question answering and entailment.

💡

Why MLM is better than left-to-right LM: GPT-style models predict the next token (left-to-right only). BERT's masked LM requires the model to use BOTH left AND right context to predict a masked token. This creates richer, truly bidirectional representations. "The [MASK] sat on the mat" — understanding it's "cat" requires looking right ("mat" suggests small animal) AND left (sentence start suggests a subject noun).

Extracting BERT Embeddings with HuggingFace

python · BERT embeddings 
# !pip install transformers torch
from transformers import BertTokenizer, BertModel
import torch

# ── Load tokeniser and model ──────────────────────────────────────
tokenizer = BertTokenizer.from_pretrained('bert-base-uncased')
model     = BertModel.from_pretrained('bert-base-uncased', output_hidden_states=True)
model.eval()  # inference mode — disables dropout

# ── Tokenise two sentences with "bank" ───────────────────────────
sentences = [
    "I went to the bank to deposit money.",
    "The river bank was covered with reeds.",
]

with torch.no_grad():
    for sent in sentences:
        inputs = tokenizer(sent, return_tensors='pt', padding=True)
        outputs = model(**inputs)

        # ── Three ways to get sentence representations ─────────────

        # 1. [CLS] token output — standard for classification tasks
        cls_embedding = outputs.last_hidden_state[:, 0, :]
        print(f"[CLS] shape: {cls_embedding.shape}")  # [1, 768]

        # 2. Mean pooling over all token embeddings — often better
        token_embeddings = outputs.last_hidden_state  # [1, seq_len, 768]
        mean_embedding = token_embeddings.mean(dim=1)
        print(f"Mean pool shape: {mean_embedding.shape}")  # [1, 768]

        # 3. All hidden states (for ELMo-style layer extraction)
        hidden_states = outputs.hidden_states   # 13 tensors (embedding + 12 layers)
        # Concatenate last 4 layers (common practitioner trick for NER):
        last4 = torch.stack(hidden_states[-4:], dim=0)
        summed = last4.sum(dim=0)            # [1, seq_len, 768]

# ── Find "bank" token and compare its embeddings ─────────────────
def get_word_embedding(sentence, word, tokenizer, model):
    inputs = tokenizer(sentence, return_tensors='pt')
    tokens = tokenizer.convert_ids_to_tokens(inputs['input_ids'][0])
    word_idx = tokens.index(word)
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = model(**inputs)
    return out.last_hidden_state[0, word_idx, :]  # [768]

bank_finance = get_word_embedding("I went to the bank to deposit money.", "bank", tokenizer, model)
bank_river   = get_word_embedding("The river bank was covered with reeds.", "bank", tokenizer, model)

# Cosine similarity — should be < 0.9 (different meanings)
cos_sim = torch.nn.functional.cosine_similarity(bank_finance.unsqueeze(0),
                                                    bank_river.unsqueeze(0))
print(ff"Cosine similarity between 'bank' (finance vs river): {cos_sim.item():.4f}")
# Typically ~0.65–0.75 (contextually distinct — BERT works!)
🔍

Which BERT layer to use? For general embeddings, the last hidden state (layer 12) works well. For NER and POS tagging, a weighted sum of the last 4 layers outperforms the final layer. For fine-tuned classification, use the [CLS] token. For semantic similarity without fine-tuning, use mean pooling of all tokens — though this is still inferior to Sentence-BERT for similarity tasks.

05 — Sentence-Level Embeddings

Sentence-BERT (SBERT)

🔗

Sentence-BERT

Reimers & Gurevych — UKP Lab, TU Darmstadt · August 2019

Why BERT Alone is Terrible for Sentence Similarity

BERT can be used for sentence similarity by either (1) using the [CLS] token or (2) mean-pooling all tokens. But these approaches produce surprisingly poor results for semantic textual similarity (STS) tasks. The SBERT paper showed that raw BERT embeddings often perform worse than GloVe averaged embeddings on STS benchmarks. Why?

⚠️

The BERT Sentence Similarity Problem: The [CLS] token and mean pooling weren't trained to produce geometrically meaningful sentence-level representations. Two sentences about completely different topics can end up with high cosine similarity. And finding the most similar sentence in a corpus of 10,000 sentences requires 10,000 BERT inference passes — with BERT-base taking ~65ms each, that's 11 minutes just to compare one query. SBERT reduces this to ~5ms total by pre-computing all embeddings.

Siamese Network Architecture

Sentence-BERT fine-tunes BERT using a Siamese network — two identical BERT encoders (sharing weights) that each process one sentence, with a pooling layer and then a similarity objective. This creates a semantic embedding space where similar sentences cluster together and dissimilar ones are far apart.

Using Sentence-BERT with sentence-transformers

python · sentence-transformers 
# !pip install sentence-transformers
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util
import numpy as np

# ── Load a Sentence-BERT model ────────────────────────────────────
# Popular model options:
# 'all-MiniLM-L6-v2'         — Fast, small, great general purpose
# 'all-mpnet-base-v2'        — Best quality (slower)
# 'paraphrase-MiniLM-L6-v2'  — Paraphrase/similarity tasks
# 'multi-qa-MiniLM-L6-cos-v1'— Semantic search, query vs passage
# 'sentence-t5-base'         — T5-based, excellent performance

model = SentenceTransformer('all-MiniLM-L6-v2')

# ── Encode sentences → dense vectors ─────────────────────────────
sentences = [
    "A man is eating food.",
    "A person is eating a meal.",
    "A cat is sitting on a mat.",
    "The financial bank approved the loan.",
    "We relaxed on the river bank.",
]

embeddings = model.encode(sentences, convert_to_tensor=True)
print(embeddings.shape)  # torch.Size([5, 384]) — MiniLM uses 384d

# ── Compute pairwise cosine similarity ───────────────────────────
cos_sim = util.cos_sim(embeddings, embeddings)
print("\nSimilarity matrix:")
for i, sent1 in enumerate(sentences):
    for j, sent2 in enumerate(sentences):
        if i < j:
            print(ff"  [{cos_sim[i][j]:.3f}] '{sent1[:35]}' vs '{sent2[:35]}'")

# ── Semantic Search ───────────────────────────────────────────────
query   = "What did the person eat?"
corpus  = ["A man is eating pizza.", "The cat sat on the roof.",
           "Someone had a sandwich for lunch.", "Dogs are great pets."]

q_emb = model.encode(query,  convert_to_tensor=True)
c_emb = model.encode(corpus, convert_to_tensor=True)

hits = util.semantic_search(q_emb, c_emb, top_k=3)
print(f"\nTop results for: '{query}'")
for hit in hits[0]:
    print(ff"  [{hit['score']:.3f}] {corpus[hit['corpus_id']]}")

# ── Clustering Example ────────────────────────────────────────────
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans

embs_np = embeddings.cpu().numpy()
kmeans  = KMeans(n_clusters=2, random_state=42).fit(embs_np)
print("\nCluster assignments:")
for sent, label in zip(sentences, kmeans.labels_):
    print(ff"  Cluster {label}: {sent}")
🟢

Choosing between SBERT models: For most use-cases, start with all-MiniLM-L6-v2 — it's small (80MB), very fast, and surprisingly good. For highest accuracy, use all-mpnet-base-v2. For multilingual tasks, use paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2. For semantic search (asymmetric: short query vs long document), use a bi-encoder model like multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1.

python · unified HuggingFace pipeline 
from transformers import pipeline, AutoTokenizer, AutoModel
import torch

# ── High-level pipeline: sentiment classification ─────────────────
classifier = pipeline("text-classification", model="distilbert-base-uncased-finetuned-sst-2-english")
result = classifier("This movie was absolutely fantastic!")
print(result)   # [{'label': 'POSITIVE', 'score': 0.9998}]

# ── High-level pipeline: NER ──────────────────────────────────────
ner = pipeline("ner", model="dbmdz/bert-large-cased-finetuned-conll03-english",
               aggregation_strategy="simple")
entities = ner("Elon Musk founded SpaceX in California.")
for e in entities:
    print(ff"  {e['word']:15s} → {e['entity_group']:5s} ({e['score']:.3f})")
# Elon Musk        → PER   (0.999)
# SpaceX           → ORG   (0.998)
# California       → LOC   (0.997)

# ── Manual embedding extraction with mean pooling ─────────────────
def mean_pooling(model_output, attention_mask):
    token_embeddings = model_output.last_hidden_state
    input_mask_expanded = attention_mask.unsqueeze(-1).expand(token_embeddings.size()).float()
    return torch.sum(token_embeddings * input_mask_expanded, 1) / \
           torch.clamp(input_mask_expanded.sum(1), min=1e-9)

tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained('sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
model     = AutoModel.from_pretrained('sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2')
model.eval()

sentences = ["The cat sat on the mat.", "A feline rested on the carpet."]
encoded   = tokenizer(sentences, padding=True, truncation=True, return_tensors='pt')

with torch.no_grad():
    outputs = model(**encoded)

sentence_embeddings = mean_pooling(outputs, encoded['attention_mask'])
# Normalise for cosine similarity
sentence_embeddings = torch.nn.functional.normalize(sentence_embeddings, p=2, dim=1)

sim = torch.mm(sentence_embeddings[0].unsqueeze(0), sentence_embeddings[1].unsqueeze(0).T)
print(ff"Similarity: {sim.item():.4f}")  # should be ~0.85+ (synonymous sentences)

The Pre-Train → Fine-Tune Paradigm

One of BERT's greatest contributions was formalising transfer learning for NLP. The workflow is always:

STEP 1
📚
Pre-Training
Massive corpus, self-supervised (MLM/NSP). Done once by Google/Meta/etc.
STEP 2
🎯
Fine-Tuning
Small labelled dataset for YOUR task. A few hours on 1 GPU. You do this.
STEP 3
🚀
Deploy
API endpoint, batch inference, or embedded in application.
🔗

Connection to later classes: BERT's pre-train → fine-tune paradigm is EXACTLY what Classes 21–24 do for LLMs. Continued Pre-Training (CPT) = domain pre-training. Instruction Fine-Tuning (SFT) = task fine-tuning. The scale is different (billions vs millions of parameters) but the principle is identical. Understanding BERT here makes the LLM fine-tuning chapters click into place.

1. What is the fundamental difference between static embeddings (Word2Vec) and contextual embeddings (BERT)?

▶ Show Answer
Static embeddings assign exactly one fixed vector per word type — the word "bank" always maps to the same vector regardless of context. Contextual embeddings read the entire input sequence before generating each token's representation, so the vector for "bank" in "financial bank" is different from "river bank." The embedding depends on the surrounding words, not just the word itself.

2. What are BERT's two pre-training objectives and what does each one teach the model?

▶ Show Answer
Masked Language Modeling (MLM): 15% of tokens are randomly masked and the model must predict the original token using both left and right context. This forces truly bidirectional attention and teaches rich word-level contextual representations. Next Sentence Prediction (NSP): The model sees two sentences and must classify whether B directly follows A or is a random sentence from the corpus. This teaches the model to understand inter-sentence relationships — important for tasks like question answering and natural language inference.

3. Why does ELMo have "shallow" bidirectionality compared to BERT's "deep" bidirectionality?

▶ Show Answer
ELMo uses two separate LSTM networks — one reading left-to-right and one reading right-to-left. Their outputs are only concatenated at the end. Each direction cannot directly attend to the other. BERT uses multi-head self-attention, where every token in a sequence can attend to every other token simultaneously — in a single layer. So in BERT, "bank" can attend to "river" AND "deposit" at the same time in each layer, creating deeply integrated bidirectional representations rather than ELMo's superficially combined unidirectional ones.

4. Why is raw BERT bad for sentence similarity, and how does Sentence-BERT fix it?

▶ Show Answer
Raw BERT was not trained to produce geometrically meaningful sentence-level embeddings. The [CLS] token and mean-pooling produce embedding spaces where cosine similarity doesn't reliably reflect semantic similarity — completely unrelated sentences can have high cosine similarity. Also, computing similarity between N sentences with BERT requires N×N inference passes (very slow at scale). Sentence-BERT fine-tunes BERT using a Siamese network with NLI/STS data and a similarity objective. This creates an embedding space where cosine similarity correctly reflects semantic relatedness. All sentence embeddings can be pre-computed once, making search over millions of documents nearly instant.

5. What is the [CLS] token in BERT and how is it used?

▶ Show Answer
The [CLS] (Classification) token is a special token prepended to every BERT input sequence. After passing through all 12 encoder layers, the output embedding at the [CLS] position serves as a pooled representation of the entire sequence. During fine-tuning for classification tasks, a linear layer is added on top of the [CLS] embedding and the entire model is trained end-to-end. Because [CLS] attends to all tokens via self-attention, it theoretically aggregates information from the whole input — though in practice, mean-pooling of all token embeddings often outperforms [CLS] for semantic tasks.

6. What problem does DistilBERT solve and how does it achieve this?

▶ Show Answer
DistilBERT solves the problem that BERT-base is too slow and large for many production deployments. It uses knowledge distillation — a smaller "student" network is trained to mimic the outputs of the larger BERT "teacher" network. DistilBERT has 6 layers instead of 12, making it 40% smaller and 60% faster while retaining 97% of BERT's performance on the GLUE benchmark. The student learns from the teacher's soft probability distributions (which carry more information than hard labels) as well as intermediate hidden state activations.

7. For a RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) system, would you use BERT or Sentence-BERT for embedding the document corpus? Why?

▶ Show Answer
Sentence-BERT (specifically a bi-encoder model like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 or multi-qa-mpnet-base-dot-v1). RAG requires embedding potentially millions of documents once, then doing fast cosine similarity lookups at query time. BERT does not produce embeddings where cosine similarity reliably reflects semantic relatedness — its embedding space is not calibrated for this. SBERT produces properly calibrated sentence embeddings where similar passages naturally cluster together. Furthermore, BERT would require re-running the full model for every document-query pair, which is computationally infeasible at scale. SBERT pre-encodes the corpus once and then queries are compared with a fast vector similarity search (using FAISS, Chroma, or Qdrant).
